Test what the model does, not only its score

A holdout error tells you how far off the forecast was on average. It does not tell you whether the forecast expects more riders at 04:00 than at 08:00, whether loading the same file twice changes it, or whether closing a station makes that station's forecast fall. Tonight we borrow three test types from NLP, adapt them to Wrenfold's demand model, write them in pytest, and then look honestly at what they cost and what they catch.

Session 4 ended with a slightly awkward result: on Wrenfold's twelve bugs, the cheapest set of six tests, costing 10 modelled units, catches everything, and none of the six ever looks at a prediction. Part 1 · covers CheckList's MFT, INV and DIR tests

Beyond the holdout number

5 min live

In 2020 Ribeiro, Wu, Guestrin and Singh argued that held-out accuracy "often overestimates the performance" of NLP models: a model can score well on a test set and still fail at simple things a user would check first. Their answer, CheckList, borrows from software testing. Instead of one number, you write small tests of specific behaviours, and each test reads predictions directly. Three test types do most of the work.

LiveThree test types from CheckList3 min▶

Minimum functionality test (MFT). Described in the paper as "inspired by unit tests in software engineering": a collection of simple examples, each with its label, that checks one behaviour. If the model cannot get the easy cases right, its overall accuracy is hiding something.

Invariance test (INV). Apply a label-preserving perturbation to the input and expect the prediction to stay the same. The test does not need to know the right answer, only that the change should not move it.

Directional expectation test (DIR). Change the input in a way whose effect has a known direction, and expect the prediction to move that way. CheckList's own example: adding a negative phrase to a text must not make its sentiment more positive. Again, no exact label is needed, only the direction.

Source: Ribeiro, M. T., Wu, T., Guestrin, C. and Singh, S. (2020). "Beyond Accuracy: Behavioral Testing of NLP Models with CheckList". ACL 2020, 4902-4912. It won the conference's Best Overall Paper award.

LiveOur adaptation to a tabular demand forecast2 min▶

CheckList is an NLP method. Applying MFT, INV and DIR to a bike-demand forecast is our adaptation, not something the paper tested, so treat the mapping as a design choice you can argue with. The translation we use for the Constructed Wrenfold example:

Test typeThe behaviour we pin on Wrenfold's forecast
MFTOn a weekday, every station's forecast for 04:00 is below its forecast for 08:00. Anyone who has seen a city at 04:00 knows the answer.
INVLoading the same source file a second time changes no prediction. A re-run is a label-preserving change to the input.
DIRIf station 030 is closed, its own forecast falls to 10% or less of what it was. The direction is known, the exact value is not.

What carries over intact is the idea that matters: each test states a behaviour a domain expert would sign, and reads the prediction to check it.

Real world

Twice the tests, almost three times the bugs. In the CheckList paper's user study, practitioners working with CheckList created twice as many tests and found almost three times as many bugs as users without it. The authors also report a team responsible for a commercial sentiment model, already extensively tested, that found new and actionable bugs once they wrote behaviour tests. The model had passed its held-out evaluation; the bugs were in behaviours the evaluation never asked about.

Part 2 · covers MFT, INV and DIR on the demand model

Three behaviour tests on Wrenfold's forecast

6 min live

Wrenfold's model is deliberately plain (Constructed Wrenfold example): for each of the five busiest stations, the average number of departures per hour of day, separately for weekdays and weekends, fitted on 1-21 May and scored on 22-31 May. Plain is good here. A behaviour test on a simple model is easy to reason about, and every number below is one the bench engine prints when it runs on the real HSL trips.

LiveMFT: 04:00 below 08:00 on a weekday, every station2 min▶

The test asks the fitted model for Monday 24 May at 04:00 and at 08:00, through the serving path, for each of the five forecast stations (030, 126, 113, 041, 021), and asserts the early hour is lower. On the clean run, station 030's forecast is 0.07 departures at 04:00 and 6.93 at 08:00: pass.

What it catches on the bench. The timezone bug, where local Helsinki times are parsed as UTC and every trip moves three hours: 030's forecast becomes 0.20 at 04:00 and 0.00 at 08:00, and the test fails. And train/serve skew, where serving computes the hour three hours earlier than training did, so the request for 08:00 reads the model's 05:00 slot. Both bugs leave every row valid; the timezone bug even leaves the holdout error essentially unchanged at 1.9098. Only a test that asks the model a question with an obvious answer notices.

LiveINV: load the file again, nothing changes1 min▶

A re-run of the load is a label-preserving change: the trips did not happen twice because a job ran twice. So the test runs the whole pipeline on the normal load and on a load where the file arrived a second time, and asserts every prediction for 22-31 May is equal to within 1e-9.

What it catches on the bench. Duplicated trips. With the de-duplication step missing, the second load doubles every count again, and every prediction changes. With de-duplication in place, the extra copies are dropped and nothing moves. The test reads predictions, but what it really pins is that the pipeline is idempotent.

LiveDIR: close station 030, its forecast must fall to 10% or less1 min▶

Remove every trip that departs station 030, re-run the pipeline, refit, and compare 030's mean forecast over 22-31 May with what it was before. The direction is certain: no departures, so the forecast must fall. The test allows anything at or below 10% of the original.

What it catches on the bench. Label shift, where the aggregation counts arrivals instead of departures. Closing 030 removes its departures, but trips from the other four stations keep ending at 030, so the arrivals it is now counting still arrive. The forecast does not fall and the test fails. The holdout error, meanwhile, drops to 0.3277: the bug looks like an improvement.

Self-studyBehaviour tests are not metrics3 min▶

Wrenfold's holdout test fails when the forecast error on 22-31 May goes above 2.2 trips per station-hour. The clean error is 1.9113. Three bugs pass that bar because they make the error lower: target leakage scores 1.7409, label shift 0.3277 and type coercion 0.9319. A metric threshold measures how close the predictions were to the targets. It has no view on whether the targets were the right thing to predict, or whether the model saw them while fitting.

A behaviour test asks a different question: does the model behave the way the domain says it must, whatever its average error? That is why the two kinds complement each other rather than compete. Choosing the metric, the split and the validation design is its own craft; it is taught in Learn Model Evaluation, a1. Here we only need the consequence: a bug that flatters the metric needs a test that does not read the metric.

Real world

Training-serving skew, as Google defines it. Martin Zinkevich's Rules of Machine Learning defines training-serving skew as "a difference between performance during training and performance during serving", and lists its causes. The first is a difference in how data is handled in the training and serving pipelines, which is exactly Wrenfold's serve-skew bug: the same hour of day computed two ways. The rules recommend re-using code between training and serving (Rule 32). The MFT is the check that tells you when you did not.

Part 3 · covers the ML Test Score and the open question

How much model testing before a model earns its keep?

4 min live

Behaviour tests are one part of testing an ML system. Breck and colleagues at Google wrote down the rest as a rubric, the ML Test Score, and gave it an unusual way of adding up. The rubric is well cited. How far down it a young model should go is not settled, and the honest move is to show you both sides.

LiveThe rubric2 min▶

The ML Test Score lists 28 specific tests and monitoring needs in four sections: features and data, model development, ML infrastructure and monitoring. A few of the items, in the paper's words: "Feature expectations are captured in a schema" opens the data list; the infrastructure list includes "The ML pipeline is integration tested" and "Model quality is validated before serving"; the monitoring list, seven tests long, includes "Training and serving are not skewed".

Scoring. Half a point for a test run manually with the results documented and distributed; a full point for a test that runs automatically on a repeated basis. Points are summed per section, and the final score is the minimum of the four section sums. The authors weight every test the same on purpose: "the relative importance of tests to teams will vary depending on their specific priorities".

Reading the number. The paper's interpretation table runs from 0, "More of a research project than a productionized system", through intermediate bands, to strong automated testing in the band above 3 and up to 5, and exceptional above 5. Read the paper for the wording of the middle bands.

Source: Breck, E., Cai, S., Nielsen, E., Salib, M. and Sculley, D. (2017). "The ML Test Score: A Rubric for ML Production Readiness and Technical Debt Reduction". IEEE Big Data 2017.

LiveThe disagreement2 min▶

Side A: test the model, and raise the weakest section. Breck and colleagues found ML-specific tests to be non-obvious and under-implemented: in their survey of several dozen teams, none of the 28 tests was implemented by more than 80% of teams. Their prescription is to score the whole system and keep raising the minimum section, because the gaps are where production surprises come from.

Side B: earn the model first. Zinkevich's Rule 1 is "Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning", and Rule 4 is "Keep the first model simple and get the infrastructure right." On this view, heavy model testing on a first model that may be replaced next quarter is spend before evidence. Breck and colleagues concede part of this themselves: their interpretation table notes that different systems at different points in their development may reasonably aim at different points on the scale.

What nobody has measured. We found no study that measures the payoff curve: how many production incidents each additional point of ML testing prevents, at what cost, for which kind of system. Without it, the right level for your model is a judgement about its stakes and its life expectancy, not a number you can look up.

Self-studyWhat the bench says, and what it cannot say3 min▶

What it says. On the session 4 bench, the step from "+ data checks" to all 17 tests adds the three behaviour tests and the holdout test, and costs 50 more modelled units (29 to 79). It catches no extra bug: 12 caught, 0 shipping, 0 false alarms before and after. Each behaviour test does catch something (the MFT catches timezone and serve skew, the INV catches duplicated trips, the DIR catches label shift), but every one of those bugs is also caught by a test in the cheapest set of six. On this list, behaviour tests add no catch.

What it cannot say. The list has twelve bugs and we planted all of them, so the cheapest set was computed knowing the answers. A real team does not know its next bug. The behaviour tests are the only tests on the bench that read predictions, which means they are the only ones that would notice a bug in a place no unit test or data check looks, such as a model change that turns the rush hour upside down while every row stays clean. Whether that insurance is worth 40 modelled units for your model is exactly the open question above.

Real world

Thirty-six teams across Google. Breck and colleagues did not write the rubric from an armchair: they met with 36 teams across Google while developing it, and surveyed several dozen. The finding that no test was implemented by more than 80% of teams came from organisations with strong general testing habits, which is the point: knowing how to test software does not tell you which tests an ML system needs.

Build-along

Three behaviour tests on Wrenfold's demand model

★ 22 min · everyone builds

We write the three tests as pytest code against the same functions the bench engine runs (Constructed Wrenfold example; the trips are real HSL rows from May 2021). Read and edit each box in the page; running it locally is Self-study. The DIR test is the one worth drawing first, because it shows why a prediction can expose what a row count cannot.

Read the model interface. This is the Python form of the bench engine's train_feat, serve_feat, fit and predict, simplified to the clean path. The model fits on 1-21 May; a prediction for any station-hour goes through the serving features, which is where the serve-skew bug lives. clean and aggregate are the session 2 and 3 functions.

★ Step 1 · wrenfold_model.py, the model interface# Constructed Wrenfold example: the forecast behind the tests FORECAST_STATIONS = ["030", "126", "113", "041", "021"] WEEKEND = {1, 2, 8, 9, 15, 16, 22, 23, 29, 30} # May 2021 weekend days TRAIN_LAST_DAY = 21 # fit on 1-21 May HOURS = 744 # 31 days x 24 hours def day_of(h): return h // 24 + 1 def train_feat(h): return (1 if day_of(h) in WEEKEND else 0, h % 24) def serve_feat(h): return train_feat(h) # the serve-skew bug computes (h - 3) % 24 here def fit(agg, last_day=TRAIN_LAST_DAY): """Average departures per station, per day type, per hour of day.""" model = {} for s, counts in agg.items(): sums = [[0.0] * 24, [0.0] * 24] cnts = [[0] * 24, [0] * 24] for d in range(1, last_day + 1): for hod in range(24): h = (d - 1) * 24 + hod dt, _ = train_feat(h) sums[dt][hod] += counts[h] cnts[dt][hod] += 1 model[s] = [[sums[dt][hod] / cnts[dt][hod] for hod in range(24)] for dt in (0, 1)] return model def predict(model, s, h): dt, hod = serve_feat(h) # serving features, never the training ones return model[s][dt][hod] def run(raw, sample): """raw trips -> clean -> station-hour counts -> fit -> predict 22-31 May""" c = clean(raw, sample) agg = aggregate(c["rows"], FORECAST_STATIONS) model = fit(agg) preds = {s: [predict(model, s, h) for h in range(TRAIN_LAST_DAY * 24, HOURS)] for s in agg} return {"model": model, "preds": preds}

Write the fixtures and the MFT. Fitting the model is the expensive part, so the clean run is a module-scoped fixture that every test shares. The MFT then asks two questions per station. Hour 23 x 24 + 4 is Monday 24 May at 04:00, a weekday inside the scored period.

★ Step 2 · conftest.py and test_mft# conftest.py import pytest from wrenfold_pipeline import load_sample, raw_rows from wrenfold_model import run @pytest.fixture(scope="module") def sample(): return load_sample("assets/tdm-sample.js") @pytest.fixture(scope="module") def clean_run(sample): return run(raw_rows(sample), sample) # the file as published: every row twice # test_behaviour.py from wrenfold_model import predict MON_04 = 23 * 24 + 4 # Monday 24 May, 04:00 MON_08 = 23 * 24 + 8 # Monday 24 May, 08:00 def test_mft_early_morning_below_rush_hour(clean_run): model = clean_run["model"] for s in clean_run["preds"]: # every forecast station assert predict(model, s, MON_04) < predict(model, s, MON_08), s

Write the INV. raw_rows(sample, copies=4) rebuilds the load as if the job had run twice: every line arrives four times instead of the file's usual two. With de-duplication working, nothing should change. The tolerance is 1e-9 because predictions are floating-point averages.

★ Step 3 · test_invfrom wrenfold_pipeline import raw_rows from wrenfold_model import run def test_inv_loading_the_file_twice_changes_nothing(sample, clean_run): reloaded = run(raw_rows(sample, copies=4), sample) # the load ran twice assert reloaded["preds"].keys() == clean_run["preds"].keys() for s, before in clean_run["preds"].items(): after = reloaded["preds"][s] assert all(abs(a - b) < 1e-9 for a, b in zip(before, after)), s

Write the DIR. drop_station removes every trip departing 030 before the pipeline sees it. The first assert guards against a vacuous pass: if 030's forecast were already 0, "falls to 10% or less" would be true and mean nothing.

★ Step 4 · test_dirCLOSED = "030" def mean(xs): return sum(xs) / len(xs) def test_dir_closing_030_lowers_its_forecast(sample, clean_run): closed = run(raw_rows(sample, drop_station=CLOSED), sample) before = mean(clean_run["preds"][CLOSED]) after = mean(closed["preds"][CLOSED]) assert before > 0 assert after <= 0.1 * before

Price them, then predict the bench. Each test re-runs some or all of the pipeline, so they are the dearest tests Wrenfold has. Before you look back at session 4's catch matrix, write down which bugs you expect each to fail on, and whether any of those bugs would ship without it.

Write the behaviour before the code that might break it. "04:00 is quieter than 08:00 on a weekday" and "closing a station lowers its forecast" are sentences an operations lead would sign without reading any Python. That is what makes a behaviour test durable: it survives a model rewrite, because it pins what the forecast must do, not how it does it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Wrenfold's holdout test fails when the forecast error goes above 2.2. With label shift planted, the error is 0.3277 against a clean 1.9113. Why can the threshold never catch this bug?

A metric compares predictions with targets; it cannot tell you the targets are the wrong thing. The DIR test, which reads what happens when a station closes, does notice.

2 · Wrenfold's INV test runs the pipeline on the normal load and on a load where the file arrived twice, and asserts the predictions are equal within 1e-9. Which bug does it catch?

A re-run is a label-preserving change: the trips did not happen twice. If the predictions move, the pipeline is not idempotent, and the missing dedup step is why.

3 · The ML Test Score sums points per section and then takes the minimum of the four sections, not the total. What does that choice do?

With a total, a team could score well with no monitoring at all. With a minimum, Wrenfold's constructed 4.0 in features and data counts for nothing while monitoring sits at 0.5.
